\begin{abstract}
The thermal Sunyaev--Zel'dovich (tSZ) signal encodes cosmological information in both individually detected clusters and the unresolved pressure field. We develop a self-consistent framework that jointly analyzes cluster number counts (CNC) and the tSZ power spectrum after the resolved clusters are masked. We show that masking suppresses the non-Gaussian covariance of the tSZ power spectrum and shifts its sensitivity from the massive, low-redshift halos towards lower-mass halos at higher redshift. We validate the framework using \textit{Planck}-like synthetic skies and study how its performance is affected by residual foregrounds. In the absence of residual foregrounds, the masked power spectrum contains substantially more cosmological information than the total power spectrum, and its combination with CNC significantly improves constraints on $\Omega_{\rm m}$ and $\sigma_8$. However, these improvements are strongly reduced after marginalizing over residual foreground amplitudes, in which case CNC dominates the cosmological constraints while the joint analysis primarily improves the determination of the foreground contributions. We then apply the framework to \textit{Planck}-like multifrequency skies from the FLAMINGO hydrodynamical simulations. At fixed cosmology, we find that astrophysical variations change the large-scale shape of the masked tSZ power spectrum. Masking reduces the non-Gaussian sample variance, making these differences potentially distinguishable, although exploiting this information requires accurate modelling of foreground residuals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================
The standard cosmological model provides a remarkably successful description of the evolution of the Universe, from the primordial perturbations generated in the early Universe to the formation of the large-scale structure observed today. These initially small perturbations are amplified by gravitational instability, with sufficiently overdense regions undergoing nonlinear collapse to form dark matter halos. Measurements of the late-time matter distribution therefore provide powerful tests of the cosmological model and the growth of structure. Galaxy clusters are the largest gravitationally bound structures in the Universe and trace the highest-density peaks of this distribution. Their mass is dominated by dark matter, while their small baryonic component enables their direct observation through electromagnetic radiation \citep{Allen2011}.

The abundance and spatial distribution of galaxy clusters encode information about the expansion history of the Universe and the growth of cosmic structure. They are particularly sensitive to the matter density parameter, $\Omega_{\rm m}$, and the present-day amplitude of matter fluctuations, $\sigma_8$, defined as the root-mean-square amplitude of the linear matter-density field smoothed on scales of $8h^{-1}$ Mpc. This information can be probed both through the abundance of individually detected clusters and through statistical measurements of the fields sourced by the full halo population \citep{Allen2011,Planck2015XXIV}.

One of the most important observational signatures of galaxy clusters is the thermal Sunyaev--Zel'dovich (tSZ) effect, produced by the inverse-Compton scattering of cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons by hot electrons in the intracluster medium (ICM) \citep{SunyaevZeldovich1972,Carlstrom2002}. The amplitude of the effect is quantified by the dimensionless Compton-$y$ parameter, which is proportional to the line-of-sight integral of the electron pressure. The tSZ surface brightness is unaffected by cosmological surface-brightness dimming, making the effect particularly well suited to detecting massive clusters over a broad redshift range \citep{Carlstrom2002}. Catalogues of tSZ-selected clusters can therefore be used to constrain cosmological parameters through the halo mass function (HMF), provided that the survey selection function and the relation between the tSZ observable and halo mass are accurately modelled \citep{Planck2015XXIV}. The Compton-$y$ field across the sky can also be constructed using component separation techniques through the multifrequency CMB observations, enabling the study of the statistical properties of the tSZ signal across the sky \citep{Planck2015XXII}.

Cosmological information from the tSZ signal can be extracted from the Compton-$y$ map. Because the Compton-$y$ field receives a large contribution from rare, massive halos, it is highly non-Gaussian \citep{Cooray2001,HillPajer2013}. Traditionally, the tSZ analysis of microwave maps is carried out using two complementary statistics. Cluster number counts (CNC) are based on the detection of individual clusters, typically by filtering the maps with templates matched to their expected pressure profiles \citep{Melin2006}. The detected clusters are supplemented by external information on the redshifts and observable--mass relation used to connect the measured observable to the true cluster masses. The tSZ power spectrum characterizes the two-point statistics of the Compton-$y$ field \citep{KomatsuSeljak2002,HillPajer2013}. The measured spectrum can be compared with a halo-model prediction to constrain cosmological and astrophysical parameters. Other summary statistics of the Compton-$y$ field have been studied (see, e.g., \citet{Sabyr:2025}) to maximally extract the information from the Compton-$y$ field.

Previous studies have explored the combination of the CNC measurements with the summary statistics of the tSZ field. \citet{Hurier:2017} and \citet{Salvati:2018} combined cluster number counts with the conventional total tSZ power spectrum, with the former additionally including the tSZ bispectrum. \citet{Rotti_2021} subsequently introduced a signal-to-noise-ratio-based masking of the Compton-$y$ map into resolved and unresolved components and analyzed the power spectrum of the unresolved field. Since the highest peaks of the Compton-$y$ field are sourced by rare, massive clusters, masking these objects reduces the additional covariance associated with the strong non-Gaussianity of the field \citep{OsatoTakada2021}. This decomposition also changes the halo populations probed by the two observables: CNC primarily retain information from individually detected, massive clusters, whereas the unresolved power spectrum receives a larger contribution from lower-mass and higher-redshift halos. \citet{Rotti_2021} demonstrated the resulting statistical benefits of masking and identified a joint analysis of CNC and the unresolved tSZ power spectrum as a natural extension of this approach, but did not implement a joint likelihood for the two observables.

However, the practical gain from combining CNC with the unresolved tSZ power spectrum is not guaranteed, and depends on several aspects of the analysis. Masking reduces the non-Gaussian covariance of the tSZ field, but simultaneously removes part of the tSZ signal while leaving residual foreground contamination largely unaffected. The cosmic infrared background (CIB) is particularly important because it traces the same underlying large-scale structure as the tSZ signal and can therefore affect both cluster detection and the reconstructed Compton-$y$ field. In addition, baryonic feedback modifies the thermal pressure distribution within halos, with an impact that depends on specific halo populations, and may potentially differ between the total and masked tSZ signal. The performance of the joint analysis therefore depends on many factors such as cluster selection, masking, foreground contamination, and component separation.

In this work, we develop a self-consistent framework for the joint analysis of CNC and the masked tSZ power spectrum. A common signal-to-noise selection is applied to the cluster catalogue and the Compton-$y$ map, ensuring that both observables are described using the same underlying halo population, observable--mass relation, and survey selection function. We first investigate this framework using synthetic \textit{Planck}-like cluster catalogues and full-sky Compton-$y$ maps generated from a common halo-model forward model. These controlled realizations allow us to identify the halo population probed by each observable and to quantify the cosmological information contained in CNC, the total and masked tSZ power spectra, and their joint combinations. We perform these comparisons both in the signal-only case and in the presence of foreground residuals.

We then test the framework using the FLAMINGO suite of large-volume cosmological hydrodynamical simulations \citep{Schaye2023}. We make multifrequency mock sky maps with correlated tSZ and CIB lightcone maps, and test cluster detection and power spectrum recovery using component separation. At fixed cosmology, we study how the CNC and masked tSZ power spectrum respond to changes in the calibrated gas fractions and stellar masses. These end-to-end tests allow us to assess the impact of correlated CIB emission on cluster selection and power-spectrum reconstruction, while also testing the sensitivity of the two observables to changes in baryonic physics.

% In contrast to analytic halo models, hydrodynamical simulations evolve dark matter and baryons self-consistently and predict the resulting distribution of thermal pressure without imposing a universal parametric pressure profile. The FLAMINGO simulation spans a range of simulation volumes, numerical resolutions, cosmologies, and calibrated galaxy-formation models, including variations in stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. It therefore provides a suitable setting in which to assess the halo-model description and investigate how cluster masking modifies the scale dependence of the tSZ power spectrum and its sensitivity to baryonic physics.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:theory} reviews the cosmological information in the tSZ power spectrum and CNC. Section~\ref{sec:modelling} describes the halo, pressure-profile, observable--mass, and survey-selection models. In Section~\ref{sec:joint_analysis}, we formulate the joint CNC and masked-power-spectrum analysis, including its covariance, and Section~\ref{sec:kernels} identifies the halo populations contributing to each observable. Section~\ref{sec:methods} presents the synthetic-data and FLAMINGO analyses. We report the results in Section~\ref{sec:results} and discuss their implications and limitations in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Cosmological information from the tSZ signal}
\label{sec:theory}
%==============================================================================
The tSZ effect is a powerful probe of both cosmology and astrophysics. On large angular scales, the signal is dominated by hot electrons in massive galaxy clusters and is therefore sensitive to the abundance of massive halos and the growth of cosmic structure. On smaller angular scales, it becomes increasingly sensitive to the internal gas distribution within clusters and groups, providing information about baryonic processes such as feedback \citep{Carlstrom2002,HillPajer2013}.

The intensity of the tSZ effect in a given direction on the sky, $\hat{\bm{n}}$, is characterized by the thermal Compton-$y$ parameter, defined as
\begin{equation}
y(\hat{\bm{n}})
=
\int
n_{\mathrm{e}}
\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{e}}}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2}
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}
\mathrm{d}s,
\label{eq:compton_y}
\end{equation}
where $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the Boltzmann constant, $\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}$ is the Thomson scattering cross section, and $m_{\mathrm{e}}$, $n_{\mathrm{e}}$, and $T_{\mathrm{e}}$ are the electron mass, number density, and temperature, respectively. The integral is performed along the physical line-of-sight distance $s$. The contribution of the tSZ effect to a sky map observed at frequency $\nu$, expressed in thermodynamic CMB temperature units, is given by
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{tSZ}}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})}
{T_{\mathrm{CMB}}}
=
g(\nu)y(\hat{\bm{n}}).
\label{eq:tsz_temperature}
\end{equation}
Neglecting relativistic corrections, the spectral function is given by $g(\nu)=x\coth(x/2)-4$, with $x=h\nu/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{CMB}})$. Here $\Delta T_{\mathrm{tSZ}}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})$ denotes the tSZ contribution to the temperature anisotropy measured in the frequency map.

A map of the Compton-$y$ parameter can be reconstructed from multifrequency observations by exploiting the characteristic spectral dependence of the tSZ signal \citep{Planck2015XXII}. The resulting Compton-$y$ map contains cumulative signal from galaxy clusters, groups, and diffuse hot gas throughout the Universe. Different summary statistics can then be extracted from this map to constrain cosmological and astrophysical parameters. The angular power spectrum provides a statistical measure of fluctuations in the Compton-$y$ field and can be modelled using the halo-model framework \citep{CooraySheth2002,KomatsuSeljak2002,HillPajer2013}. Individual galaxy clusters can also be resolved using the Multi-Frequency Matched Filter (MMF) approach, allowing the abundance of tSZ-selected clusters to be measured \citep{Melin2006}.

\subsection{tSZ Power Spectrum}
The angular power spectrum of the Compton-$y$ map can be computed from its spherical harmonic coefficients as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{C}_{\ell}^{yy}
    =
    \frac{1}{2\ell+1}
    \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}
    \left|y_{\ell m}\right|^2,
    \label{eq:tsz_power_spectrum_estimator}
\end{equation}
where $y_{\ell m}$ denotes the spherical harmonic coefficient of the Compton-$y$ field. Within the halo-model framework, the tSZ power spectrum can be written as the sum of one-halo and two-halo contributions,
\begin{equation}
C_{\ell}^{yy}
=
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{1h}}
+
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{2h}}.
\label{eq:tsz_halo_model_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The one-halo term describes correlations sourced by gas within the same halo, whereas the two-halo term accounts for correlations between gas in two different halos.

The one-halo contribution can be understood as the population average of the squared harmonic-space pressure profile of individual clusters, given by
\begin{equation}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{1h}}
=
\left\langle
\left|
y_{\ell}(M,z;\zeta_{\rm obs})
\right|^2
\right\rangle_{\mathrm{halos}}.
\label{eq:1haloavg}
\end{equation}
Here $y_{\ell}(M,z,\zeta_{\rm obs})$ denotes the line-of-sight projected Fourier transform of the three-dimensional halo profile for a halo of mass $M$ and redshift $z$,
\begin{equation}
y_{\ell}(M,z)
=
\frac{4\pi r_{\mathrm{s}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{s}}^2}
\int_{0}^{\infty}
\mathrm{d}x
x^2 p(x;M,z)
\frac{\sin\left(\ell x/\ell_{\mathrm{s}}\right)}
{\ell x/\ell_{\mathrm{s}}},
\label{eq:projected_pressure_transform}
\end{equation}
where $x=r/r_{\mathrm{s}}$, $\ell_{\mathrm{s}}=D_{\mathrm{A}}(z)/r_{\mathrm{s}}$, $r_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the characteristic scale radius of the halo and $p(x;M,z)$ is its three-dimensional electron pressure profile. The variable $\zeta_{\rm obs}$ represents an additional observable or selection variable associated with the halo such as the observed tSZ signal-to-noise ratio $q$. For convenience, we also introduce the notation, $\langle X \rangle$, to denote the average of the quantity $X$ weighted by the halo population. For the full halo population the one-halo term is
therefore given by
\begin{align}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{1h}}
={}&
\int_{0}^{z_{\mathrm{max}}}
\mathrm{d}z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\nonumber\\
&\times
\int_{M_{\mathrm{min}}}^{M_{\mathrm{max}}}
\mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}
\left|
y_{\ell}(M,z)
\right|^2,
\label{eq:tsz_one_halo_full}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{d}V/(\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega)$ is the differential
comoving volume element per unit redshift and solid angle while
$\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}/\mathrm{d}M$ is the halo mass function (HMF).

The two-halo contribution arises from large-scale clustering of the dark matter halos. It describes the correlation between the spatial positions of the halos, given by 
\begin{equation}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{2h}}
=
\left\langle
b_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)
y_{\ell}(M,z,\zeta_{\rm obs})
\right\rangle_{\mathrm{halos}}^2
P_{\mathrm{lin}}(k_{\ell},z).
\label{eq:tsz_two_halo}
\end{equation}
where $P_{\mathrm{lin}}(k_{\ell},z)$ is the linear matter power spectrum evaluated at $k_{\ell}=(\ell+1/2)/\chi(z)$, and $b_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)$ is the linear halo bias. For the full halo population this expression becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{2h}}
={}&
\int_{0}^{z_{\mathrm{max}}}
\mathrm{d}z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\\
&\times
\left[
\int_{M_{\mathrm{min}}}^{M_{\mathrm{max}}}
\mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}
b_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)
y_{\ell}(M,z)
\right]^2
\\
&\times
P_{\mathrm{lin}}(k_{\ell},z).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tsz_two_halo_full}
\end{equation}
We adopt the linear halo bias of \citet{Tinker:2010},
\begin{equation}
b_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)
=
1
-
A\,
\frac{\nu^{a}}{\nu^{a}+\delta_{\mathrm{c}}^{a}}
+
\mathcal{B}\,\nu^{b}
+
C\,\nu^{c},
\label{eq:tinker_bias}
\end{equation}
where $\nu=\delta_{\mathrm{c}}/\sigma(M,z)$ is the peak height with
$\delta_{\mathrm{c}}\simeq 1.686$, and the coefficients
$(A,a,\mathcal{B},b,C,c)$ are the \citet{Tinker:2010} fits as a function of
the spherical-overdensity threshold (converted to a mean-matter definition
in the \texttt{hmfast} implementation used here).

\subsection{Cluster Number Counts}
Galaxy clusters detected through their tSZ signal provide a complementary cosmological observable to the tSZ power spectrum \citep{Planck2015XXIV,ZubeldiaBolliet2024}. The expected number of clusters observed by a survey in a redshift interval $[z_i,z_{i+1}]$ is given by
\begin{equation}
N_i
=
\int_{z_i}^{z_{i+1}}
\mathrm{d}z
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}z},
\label{eq:cluster_counts_redshift_bin}
\end{equation}
where the differential number count is given by
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}z}
=
\int \mathrm{d}\Omega
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\int \mathrm{d}M
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}}{\mathrm{d}M}(M,z)
\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm{n}}),
\label{eq:dNdz}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm{n}})$ denotes the completeness function which gives the probability that a cluster of mass $M$ and redshift $z$, located in the direction $\hat{\bm{n}}$, is detected by the survey.

For a catalogue selected using an observed quantity $\zeta_{\mathrm{obs}}$ and assuming that a redshift measurement is available for each detected cluster, the expected number of clusters in the redshift bin $[z_i,z_{i+1}]$ and observable bin $[\zeta_j,\zeta_{j+1}]$ is
\begin{equation}
    N_{ij}
    =
    \int_{z_i}^{z_{i+1}}
    \mathrm{d}z
    \int_{\zeta_j}^{\zeta_{j+1}}
    \mathrm{d}\zeta_{\mathrm{obs}}\,
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2N}
    {\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{\mathrm{obs}}}.
    \label{eq:cluster_counts_binned}
\end{equation}
The differential number counts are given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2N}
    {\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\zeta_{\mathrm{obs}}}
    ={}&
    \int \mathrm{d}\Omega\,
    \frac{\mathrm{d}V}
    {\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
    \\
    &\times
    \int \mathrm{d}M\,
    \frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)}
    {\mathrm{d}M}
    P\left(
        \zeta_{\mathrm{obs}}
        \mid M,z,\hat{\bm{n}}
    \right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cluster_counts_differential}
\end{equation}
Here $P(\zeta_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid M,z,\hat{\bm{n}})$ is the probability distribution of the observed selection quantity for a halo of mass $M$ at redshift $z$ and sky position $\hat{\bm{n}}$. It incorporates the tSZ mass--observable relation, intrinsic scatter, measurement noise, and spatial variations in the survey sensitivity. 

% For tSZ experiments, clusters are detected through their tSZ signal-to-noise ratio, $q$, given by 
% \begin{equation}
%     q(M,z) = \frac{y_0(M,z)}{\sigma_{y_0}(\theta_{500}, \hat{\bm{n}})},
%     \label{eq:snr_mean}
% \end{equation}
% where $y_0$ is the Compton-$y$ value evaluated at the cluster center, $\theta_{500} = r_{500c}/d_A$, and $\sigma_{y_0}$ is the noise from matched filter detection. 
For tSZ experiments, clusters are detected through their tSZ signal-to-noise ratio, $q$ \citep{Planck2015XXIV}. The clusters are subsequently assigned a measured redshift, $z$, through external probes. The cluster number count, $N(q,z)$, is therefore expressed in terms of these two observables. Following Equation \ref{eq:cluster_counts_differential}, the distribution of clusters in redshift and signal-to-noise can be written as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}N}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}q}
={}&
\int
\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\rm mask}\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\\
&\times
\int \mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}\,
P\!\left[
q\mid\bar{q}_m(M,z,\hat{\bm{n}})
\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cluster_counts_qz}
\end{equation}
where $P\!\left[q\mid\bar{q}_m(M,z,\hat{\bm{n}})\right]$ is the distribution of $q$ given the mean signal-to-noise ratio $\bar{q}_m(M,z,\hat{\bm{n}})$, predicted by the model for a cluster of mass $M$ and redshift $z$ located at sky position $\hat{\bm{n}}$ (the scaling relation). Meanwhile, $\bar{q}_m$ can be defined as the ratio of the mean tSZ signal, $\bar{y}_0$, which is the Compton-$y$ value evaluated at the cluster center, and the noise from the MMF detection, $\sigma_{y_0}(\bar{\theta}_{500},\hat{\bm{n}})$,
\begin{equation}
\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
=
\frac{
\bar y_{0}(M,z)
}{
\sigma_{y_{0}}
\left[
\bar\theta_{500}(M,z),\hat{\bm n}
\right]
}.
\label{eq:mean_snr}
\end{equation}
Note that the filter noise $\sigma_{y_0}$ depends on the sky location $\hat{\bm{n}}$ and the cluster angular size, $\theta_{500}$, which introduces additional dependence on mass and redshift. 

For clusters detected above a signal-to-noise threshold, $q_{\rm cat}$, the redshift distribution of detected clusters is obtained by integrating Equation \ref{eq:cluster_counts_differential} distribution over $q>q_{\rm cat}$ with $\zeta_{\rm obs}=q$. Following Equation \ref{eq:dNdz}, the completeness function can be written as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
=
\int_{q_{\mathrm{cat}}}^{\infty}
\mathrm{d}q\,
P\left[
q\mid \bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right].
\label{eq:cluster_completeness}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the completeness function can be expressed in terms of the underlying cluster observables. It is necessary to consider the uncertainty in observed cluster quantities and the survey selection function. Hence, Equation \ref{eq:cluster_completeness} can also be written as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
={}&
\int \mathrm{d}\ln y_{0}
\int \mathrm{d}\theta_{500}\,
P\left(
\ln y_{0},\theta_{500}\mid M,z
\right)
\\
&\times
\chi\left(
y_{0},\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n}
\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:effective_completeness}
\end{equation}
where $P\left(\ln y_{0},\theta_{500}\mid M,z \right)$ is the distribution of the intrinsic cluster observables at fixed mass and redshift, and $\chi\left(
y_{0},\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n} \right)$ is the survey selection function.

\subsection{Extracting tSZ observables from multifrequency maps}
The two tSZ observables considered in this work are usually extracted from multifrequency microwave maps. In addition to the tSZ signal, these maps contain contributions from the primary CMB, astrophysical foregrounds, and instrumental noise. Separating the tSZ signal from these components requires exploiting its distinct frequency dependence across the observed channels.

We write the observed multifrequency sky maps as a vector $\bm{d}(\hat{\bm{n}})$ with one entry per frequency channel. For $N_\nu$ channels and $N_{\rm fg}$ non-tSZ components,
\begin{equation}
\bm{d}(\hat{\bm{n}})
=
\bm{a}\,y(\hat{\bm{n}})
+
\bm{B}\,\bm{f}(\hat{\bm{n}})
+
\bm{n}(\hat{\bm{n}}),
\label{eq:multifrequency_data_model}
\end{equation}
where $y(\hat{\bm{n}})$ is the underlying Compton-$y$ field. The vectors $\bm{d}$, $\bm{a}$, and $\bm{n}$ each have shape $N_\nu$, and $\bm{a}$ is the tSZ spectral response. The matrix
$\bm{B}=(\bm{b}_1,\ldots,\bm{b}_{N_{\rm fg}})$
has shape $N_\nu\times N_{\rm fg}$: its columns are the spectral responses of the non-tSZ components, and $\bm{f}(\hat{\bm{n}})$ is the spatial amplitude of the components with shape $N_{\rm fg}$. The vector $\bm{n}(\hat{\bm{n}})$ denotes instrumental noise. 

In harmonic space, $\bm{d}_{\ell m}$ is the same $N_\nu$-vector of frequency maps. A Compton-$y$ map can be reconstructed using a harmonic internal linear combination (HILC),
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\ell m}
=
\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{d}_{\ell m},
\label{eq:hilc_estimator}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{w}_{\ell}$ is the weight vector of shape $N_\nu$. The weights are chosen to minimize the variance of the reconstructed field while preserving unit response to the tSZ signal, $\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{a}=1$. The minimum-variance HILC weights are
\begin{equation}
\bm{w}_{\ell}
=
\frac{
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}\bm{a}
}{
\bm{a}^{T}\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}\bm{a}
},
\label{eq:hilc_weights}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{C}_{\ell}$ is the $N_\nu\times N_\nu$ frequency--frequency covariance matrix of the multifrequency maps at multipole $\ell$.

In some cases, certain foreground components can bias the reconstruction of the signal of interest, particularly when they are strongly correlated with it. In such cases, a constrained ILC may be used. If the SED of a foreground is known, an additional constraint can be imposed on the ILC weights such that the response to this component vanishes while preserving unit response to the signal of interest, at the cost of increasing the variance of the reconstructed map. For a foreground component with spectral response $\bm{b}$, the weights are required to satisfy $\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{a}=1$ and $\bm{w}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{b}=0$. Minimizing the variance subject to these constraints gives
\begin{equation}
\bm{w}_{\ell}
=
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}\bm{A}
\left(
\bm{A}^{T}\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}\bm{A}
\right)^{-1}
\bm{e},
\label{eq:constrained_hilc_weights}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{A}=(\bm{a},\bm{b})$, and $\bm{e}=(1,0)^{T}$.

Resolved clusters can instead be detected directly from the multifrequency maps using a multi-frequency matched filter (MMF) \citep{Melin2006,2023MNRAS.522.5123Z}. We model the flat-sky Fourier transform of a cluster's Compton-$y$ profile as $y_{\rm cl}(\bm{\ell})=y_0\,y_{\rm t}(\bm{\ell})$, where $y_0$ is the central Compton-$y$ amplitude and $y_{\rm t}(\bm{\ell})$ is a frequency-independent spatial template. In the flat-sky approximation, the MMF estimator for $y_0$ is
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_0^{\rm MMF}
=
\left(N^{\rm MMF}\right)^{-1}
\int
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\bm{\ell}}{(2\pi)^2}\,
y_{\rm t}^{*}(\bm{\ell})\,
\bm{a}^{T}
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}
\bm{d}(\bm{\ell}),
\label{eq:mmf_estimator}
\end{equation}
with normalization
\begin{equation}
N^{\rm MMF}
=
\int
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\bm{\ell}}{(2\pi)^2}\,
|y_{\rm t}(\bm{\ell})|^2\,
\bm{a}^{T}
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}
\bm{a},
\label{eq:mmf_normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\ell=|\bm{\ell}|$ and $\bm{C}_{\ell}$ is the same covariance as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hilc_weights}. The variance of the recovered amplitude is $\sigma_{y_0}^2=(N^{\rm MMF})^{-1}$, and the detection signal-to-noise ratio is
\begin{equation}
\hat{q}
=
\frac{\hat{y}_0^{\rm MMF}}{\sigma_{y_0}}.
\label{eq:mmf_snr}
\end{equation}

Similar to the constrained ILC, foreground contamination in cluster detection can be mitigated with a spectrally constrained MMF (scMMF). When the SED of a contaminating foreground is known, the filter is constructed with the same constraints, $\bm{w}^{T}\bm{a}=1$ and $\bm{w}^{T}\bm{b}=0$. This suppresses the foreground-induced bias in the detected signal-to-noise ratio at the cost of increasing the MMF variance. The scMMF estimator for the central Compton-$y$ amplitude is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}_0^{\rm scMMF}
={}&
\left(N^{\rm scMMF}\right)^{-1}
\int
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\bm{\ell}}{(2\pi)^2}\,
y_{\rm t}^{*}(\bm{\ell})
\\
&\times
\frac{
\bm{e}^{T}
\left[
\bm{A}^{T}
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}
\bm{A}
\right]^{-1}
\bm{A}^{T}
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}
\bm{d}(\bm{\ell})
}{
\bm{e}^{T}
\left[
\bm{A}^{T}
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}
\bm{A}
\right]^{-1}
\bm{e}
},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:scmmf_estimator}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{e}$ are the same constraint matrix and unit vector as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constrained_hilc_weights}. The normalization is
\begin{equation}
N^{\rm scMMF}
=
\int
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\bm{\ell}}{(2\pi)^2}\,
\frac{
|y_{\rm t}(\bm{\ell})|^2
}{
\bm{e}^{T}
\left[
\bm{A}^{T}
\bm{C}_{\ell}^{-1}
\bm{A}
\right]^{-1}
\bm{e}
}.
\label{eq:scmmf_normalization}
\end{equation}
% and the scMMF signal-to-noise ratio is Eq.~\eqref{eq:mmf_snr} with $\hat{y}_0^{\rm MMF}$ replaced by $\hat{y}_0^{\rm scMMF}$ and $\sigma_{y_0}^2=(N^{\rm scMMF})^{-1}$. The iterative implementation of this filter, used for the FLAMINGO catalogues below, is the sciMMF.


\section{Modelling the tSZ Observables}
\label{sec:modelling}
In this section, we specify the theoretical ingredients required to model the tSZ power spectrum and CNC. This includes the halo mass function, intracluster pressure profile, observable--mass relations, and survey selection function.

% The central idea is to divide the halo population according to the cluster-selection criterion. Clusters that satisfy the detection threshold are included in the cluster catalogue and masked from the Compton-$y$ map while objects below the threshold remain in the map and contribute to the measured tSZ power spectrum. The two observables therefore probe complementary and non-overlapping halo populations.
\subsection{Halo Mass Function}
We define the cluster mass as $M_{500\mathrm{c}}$, which is the mass enclosed within a radius $R_{500\mathrm{c}}$ where the mean interior density is 500 times the critical density of the Universe at the cluster redshift:
\begin{equation}
    M_{500\mathrm{c}}
    =
    \frac{4\pi}{3}
    R_{500\mathrm{c}}^3
    500\rho_{\mathrm{c}}(z).
    \label{eq:m500c_definition}
\end{equation}
Here
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\mathrm{c}}(z)
    =
    \frac{3H^2(z)}{8\pi G}
    \label{eq:critical_density}
\end{equation}
is the critical density at redshift $z$, and $H(z)$ is the Hubble parameter.

We model the abundance of halos using the HMF given by \citet{Tinker:2008ff} evaluated for the $M_{500\mathrm{c}}$ mass definition.
The differential halo abundance is written as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\mathrm{h}}}{\mathrm{d}M_{500\mathrm{c}}}
    =
    f(\sigma,z)
    \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{m},0}}{M_{500\mathrm{c}}}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln \sigma^{-1}}
    {\mathrm{d}M_{500\mathrm{c}}},
    \label{eq:tinker_hmf}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{\mathrm{m},0}$ is the mean matter density at $z=0$, and $\sigma(M,z)$ is the standard deviation of the linear density field smoothed within a sphere of radius $R =\left[3M/(4\pi\rho_{\mathrm{m},0})\right]^{1/3}$. The multiplicity function
$f(\sigma,z)$ is parametrized as
\begin{equation}
f(\sigma,z)
=
A(z)
\left[
\left(\frac{\sigma}{b(z)}\right)^{-a(z)}
+ 1
\right]
\exp\left(-\frac{c}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:tinker_multiplicity}
\end{equation}
where the parameters $A$, $a$, $b$, and $c$ are evaluated using the fitting relations of \citet{Tinker:2008ff}.

\subsection{Pressure Profile}
The Compton-$y$ parameter is proportional to the pressure integrated along the line of sight. In this work, we model the pressure profile of clusters using the generalized Navarro-Frenk-White (GNFW) pressure profile of the form
\begin{equation}
    p(x;M,z) = C(M,z) \,
    \frac{(c_{500}x)^{-\gamma}}
    {\left[1 + (c_{500}x)^\alpha\right]^{(\gamma-\beta)/\alpha}},
    \label{eq:gnfw}
\end{equation}
with $x = r/r_s$ and a normalization $C(M,z)$ depending on mass and redshift. Following \citet{Arnaud2010}, we adopt the normalization factor as
\begin{align}
    C(M_{500\mathrm{c}},z)
    ={}& 13.41
    \left(\frac{h}{0.7}\right)^2
    \left(\frac{H(z)}{H_0}\right)^{8/3}
    \nonumber\\
    &\times
    \left[
        \frac{M_{500\mathrm{c}}h/B}
        {0.7\times 3\times10^{14}\,M_\odot}
    \right]^{2/3+0.12}
    \,\mathrm{eV\,cm^{-3}},
    \label{eq:pressure_normalisation}
\end{align}
where $B\equiv 1/(1-b)$ is the hydrostatic mass-bias parameter,
such that the hydrostatic mass is
$M_{500\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{HSE}}=M_{500\mathrm{c}}/B$.

\subsection{Observable-mass relations}
\label{subsec:obs-massrelations}
An important part of the cluster count model is the mapping between the cluster observables to the halo mass and redshift \citep{Planck2015XXIV,ZubeldiaBolliet2024}. This mapping is described by a conditional probability distribution $P(q\mid M_{500\mathrm{c}},z)$, which gives the probability of observing
a signal-to-noise ratio $q$ for a halo of mass $M_{500\mathrm{c}}$ at
redshift $z$. The mean value of signal-to-noise ratio, $\bar{q}_m$, is specified by scaling relations $\bar{y}_0(M_{500c},z)$ and $\bar{\theta}_{500}(M_{500c},z)$.

We relate the mean tSZ signal $\bar{y}_0$ to the cluster mass and redshift via
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bar{y}_0(M_{500\mathrm{c}},z)
={}&
10^{A_{\mathrm{SZ}}}
\left[
    \frac{M_{500\mathrm{c}}h/B}
    {0.7\times 3\times 10^{14}\,M_\odot}
\right]^{\alpha_{\mathrm{SZ}}}
\\
&\times
\left(\frac{H(z)}{H_0}\right)^2
\left(\frac{h}{0.7}\right)^{-1/2},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:y0_scaling_relation}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\rm SZ}$ parameterizes the tSZ signal amplitude and $\alpha_{\rm SZ}$ its mass dependence. The mean angular size of the cluster is given by 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bar{\theta}_{500}(M_{500\mathrm{c}},z)
={}&
\theta_\star
\left(\frac{h}{0.7}\right)^{-2/3}
\left[
    \frac{M_{500\mathrm{c}}/B}{3\times10^{14}\,M_\odot}
\right]^{1/3}
\\
&\times
\left(\frac{H(z)}{H_0}\right)^{-2/3}
\left[\frac{D_A(z)}{500\,{\rm Mpc}}\right]^{-1},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:theta500_scaling_relation}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_\star = 6.997\,\mathrm{arcmin}$.

At fixed mass and redshift, the tSZ signal varies between clusters due to differences in their thermodynamic properties. We account for this cluster-to-cluster variation by introducing an intrinsic log-normal scatter around the mean observable-mass relation. Specifically, we assume that $y_0(M,z)$ follows a log-normal distribution about its mean value $\bar{y}_0(M,z)$ with logarithmic scatter $\sigma_{\ln Y}$, and we neglect scatter in the cluster angular size by assuming that $\theta_{500}$ is fixed at its mean value $\bar{\theta}_{500}(M,z)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P\!\left(\ln y_0,\theta_{500}\mid M,z\right)
={}&
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\ln Y}}\,
e^{-\left[\ln y_0-\ln \bar{y}_0(M,z)\right]^2/
\left(2\sigma_{\ln Y}^{2}\right)}
\\
&\times
\delta_{\mathrm D}\!\left[
\theta_{500}-\bar{\theta}_{500}(M,z)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:intrinsic_observable_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{\mathrm{D}}$ is the Dirac delta function. 

The distribution of the measured signal-to-noise ratio can then be written as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&P\left[
q\mid \bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right]
\\
&\quad =
\int \mathrm{d}\ln q_m\,
P\left(q\mid q_m\right)
P\left[
\ln q_m
\mid
\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q_distribution_convolution}
\end{equation}
where $P\left(q\mid q_m\right)$ is the distribution of observed signal-to-noise, $q$, conditioned on the underlying model value $q_m$, and $P\left[\ln q_m\mid\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right]$ represents the intrinsic cluster-to-cluster scatter. Since the intrinsic scatter is introduced only in $y_0$, while
$\theta_{500}$ is fixed at $\bar{\theta}_{500}(M,z)$ in our model, the distribution
of $q_m$ is also log-normal:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&P\left[
\ln q_m
\mid
\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right]
\\
&\quad =
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\ln Y}}\,
e^{
-\ln^2\left[
q_m/\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right]/
\left(2\sigma_{\ln Y}^{2}\right)
}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:intrinsic_qm_distribution}
\end{equation}
Substituting Equations \ref{eq:q_distribution_convolution} and \ref{eq:intrinsic_qm_distribution} into Equations \ref{eq:cluster_completeness} and \ref{eq:effective_completeness}, the survey selection function can be written as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\chi\left(
y_0,\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n}
\right)
\\
&\quad =
\int_{q_{\rm cat}}^{\infty}
\mathrm{d}q\,
P\left[
q\mid
q_m(y_0,\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n})
\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:survey_selection_observables}
\end{equation}
It represents the probability that a cluster with intrinsic observables is detected above the signal-to-noise threshold, $q_{\rm cat}$. Averaging this quantity over the intrinsic observable distribution at fixed mass and redshift then recovers the completeness function $\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm n})$.

\subsection{Survey selection function}
The survey selection function \citep{Planck2015XXIV,ZubeldiaBolliet2024} is described by
$P\left[q\mid q_m(y_0,\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n})\right]$, which gives the probability distribution of the observed signal-to-noise ratio $q$ for a cluster with intrinsic observables $y_0$ and $\theta_{500}$ at sky position $\hat{\bm n}$. We assume that the measured signal-to-noise ratio, $q$, follows a Gaussian distribution centered on $q_m$, such that $P(q\mid q_m)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-(q-q_m)^2/2}$. The survey selection function is then given by the error function,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\chi\left(
y_0,\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n}
\right)
={}&
\int_{q_{\rm cat}}^{\infty}
\mathrm{d}q\,
P\left[
q\mid q_m(y_0,\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n})
\right]
\\
={}&
\frac{1}{2}
\operatorname{erfc}\left[
\frac{
q_{\rm cat}
-
q_m(y_0,\theta_{500},\hat{\bm n})
}
{\sqrt{2}}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:survey_selection_function}
\end{equation}

Combining the log-normal intrinsic scatter model in Section \ref{subsec:obs-massrelations} and the error function survey selection function, the completeness function can be written as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
={}&
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\mathrm{d}u\,
e^{-u^2/2}
\\
&\times
\operatorname{erfc}\left[
\frac{
q_{\rm cat}
-
\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
e^{\sigma_{\ln Y}u}
}
{\sqrt{2}}
\right], 
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:completeness_standard_normal}
\end{equation}
where $u=\ln[q_m/\bar q_m]/\sigma_{\ln Y}$.

\section{Joint Analysis of Cluster Counts and the Masked tSZ Power Spectrum}
\label{sec:joint_analysis}
Having presented the theoretical ingredients, we now develop a self-consistent framework for combining the tSZ power spectrum and CNC within a single analysis. We decompose the Compton-$y$ map into resolved and unresolved components according to the signal-to-noise threshold $q_{\rm cat}$. Clusters detected with $q\geq q_{\rm cat}$ constitute the resolved population: they enter the cluster number count sample and are masked from the map. Clusters with $q<q_{\rm cat}$ form the unresolved population and remain in the map, contributing to the masked tSZ power spectrum. The two observables therefore probe complementary subsets of the halo populations. Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} illustrates this
decomposition for a synthetic full-sky Compton-$y$ realization with
$q_{\rm cat}=5$, together with the corresponding masked power spectrum and binned CNC in redshift and signal-to-noise \citep{Rotti_2021,OsatoTakada2021}.

\subsection{Cluster Number Counts}
We bin the resolved clusters in redshift and signal-to-noise bins. The expected number of detected clusters in the redshift bin
$[z_i,z_{i+1}]$ and signal-to-noise bin $[q_j,q_{j+1}]$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
N_{ij}
={}&
\int_{z_i}^{z_{i+1}}
\mathrm{d}z
\int_{\Omega_{\rm mask}}
\mathrm{d}\Omega\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\\
&\times
\int_{M_{\rm min}}^{M_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}
\mathcal{P}_j(M,z,\hat{\bm n}),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:joint_cluster_counts}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}_j(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
=
\int_{q_j}^{q_{j+1}}
\mathrm{d}q\,
P\left[
q\mid\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
\right],
\label{eq:q_bin_probability}
\end{equation}
and the lower boundary of the bins satisfies $q_j\geq q_{\rm cat}$.

\subsection{Masked tSZ Power Spectrum}
The complementary unresolved population contributes to the masked tSZ
power spectrum. We note that, in the presence of intrinsic scatter, the probability that a cluster remains below the detection threshold and the
amplitude of its contribution to the tSZ power spectrum are correlated. The masked power spectrum therefore cannot be obtained by simply weighting the mean cluster profile by the unresolved
fraction, $1-\hat{\chi}$. Although
this correction is expected to be small for the intrinsic scatter adopted in our analysis, we include it for consistency.

To account for this effect, we define the $n$th selection-weighted moment
of the unresolved population as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
W_n(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
={}&
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\mathrm{d}u\,
e^{-u^2/2}
e^{n\sigma_{\ln Y}u}
\\
&\times
\operatorname{erfc}\left[
\frac{
\bar q_m(M,z,\hat{\bm n})
e^{\sigma_{\ln Y}u}
-q_{\rm cat}
}
{\sqrt{2}}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:unresolved_moment_weight}
\end{equation}
Here the factor $e^{n\sigma_{\ln Y}u}$ accounts for the change in the profile amplitude induced by the log-normal intrinsic scatter. Note that $1-W_0(M,z,\hat{\bm n})=\hat{\chi}(M,z,\hat{\bm n})$
is the completeness function.

The one-halo contribution to the masked power spectrum is then given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{1h,mask}}
={}&
\int_{0}^{z_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\int_{M_{\rm min}}^{M_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}
\\
&\times
\left|\bar{y}_{\ell}(M,z)\right|^2
W_2(M,z).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:masked_tsz_1h}
\end{equation}
The second-moment weight $W_2$ appears because the contribution of an individual halo to the one-halo power spectrum is proportional to
$|y_\ell|^2$, as given by Equation \ref{eq:1haloavg}. Note that in the absence of masking, corresponding to
$q_{\rm cat}\rightarrow\infty$, all halos remain in the Compton-$y$
map and the moment weight reduces to
$W_n\rightarrow e^{n^2\sigma_{\ln Y}^2/2}$.
so that the one-halo power spectrum becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{1h,full}}
={}&
e^{2\sigma_{\ln Y}^{2}}
\int_{0}^{z_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\\
&\times
\int_{M_{\rm min}}^{M_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}
\left|\bar y_{\ell}(M,z)\right|^{2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:full_tsz_1h_scatter}
\end{equation}
Thus, without masking, the log-normal intrinsic scatter produces a
uniform boost of $e^{2\sigma_{\ln Y}^{2}}$ on the one-halo tSZ power spectrum across all multipoles. 

Similarly, the two-halo contribution is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{2h,mask}}
={}&
\int_{0}^{z_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
P_{\rm lin}(k_\ell,z)
\\
&\times
\Bigg[
\int_{M_{\rm min}}^{M_{\rm max}}
\mathrm{d}M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}(M,z)}
{\mathrm{d}M}
\\
&\qquad\quad\times
b_{\rm h}(M,z)\,
\bar{y}_{\ell}(M,z)\,
W_1(M,z)
\Bigg]^2 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:masked_tsz_2h}
\end{equation}
The first-moment weight $W_1$ appears because each of the two distinct
halos contributes one factor of $y_\ell$ to the two-halo correlation.
The total masked tSZ power spectrum is then
\begin{equation}
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{mask}}
=
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{1h,mask}}
+
C_{\ell}^{yy,\mathrm{2h,mask}}.
\label{eq:masked_tsz_total}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:tsz_ps_snr_masking} shows the shape dependence of the tSZ power spectrum in terms of $q_{\rm cat}$. Masking giant clusters suppresses the large-scale one-halo power most strongly, while small scales are less affected until the threshold becomes severe. Note that the two-halo term remains subdominant for the masked power spectrum on large scales.

\subsection{Masked Trispectrum and Covariance}
\label{sec:masked_covariance}
The covariance of the tSZ power spectrum includes both the disconnected Gaussian contribution and the connected four-point contribution described by the tSZ trispectrum. When massive clusters are retained in the Compton-$y$ map, the latter is dominated by the one-halo trispectrum \citep{Cooray2001,KomatsuSeljak2002,HillPajer2013,OsatoTakada2021}, given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_{\ell\ell'}^{yy,\mathrm{mask}}
={}&
\int \mathrm{d}z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}
{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
\int \mathrm{d}\ln M\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}}
{\mathrm{d}\ln M}
\\
&\times
\left|\bar y_{\ell}(M,z)\right|^2
\left|\bar y_{\ell'}(M,z)\right|^2
W_4(M,z).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:masked_trispectrum}
\end{equation}
The fourth-moment weight $W_4$ appears because the same halo contributes two powers of the profile amplitude to each of the two bandpowers.

For the bandpower covariance, we define the bin-averaged trispectrum as
\begin{equation}
\bar T_{bb'}^{yy,\mathrm{mask}}
=
\frac{1}
{|\mathcal{B}_b|\,|\mathcal{B}_{b'}|}
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{B}_b}
\sum_{\ell'\in\mathcal{B}_{b'}}
T_{\ell\ell'}^{yy,\mathrm{mask}},
\label{eq:binned_masked_trispectrum}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}_b$ denotes the set of integer multipoles contained
in bin $b$. Neglecting instrumental noise, the covariance of the masked bandpowers is then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
M_{bb'}^{yy,\mathrm{mask}}
={}&
\delta_{bb'}
\frac{
2\left(
C_b^{yy,\mathrm{mask}}
\right)^2
}
{(2\ell_b+1)\,f_{\rm sky}\,\Delta\ell_b}
\\
&+
\frac{
\bar T_{bb'}^{yy,\mathrm{mask}}
}
{4\pi f_{\rm sky}} .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:masked_bandpower_covariance}
\end{equation}

\section{Halo contributions and the impact of masking}
\label{sec:kernels}
The tSZ power spectrum and the CNC are sourced by halos with various mass and redshift. The total tSZ power spectrum is particularly sensitive to rare, massive, low-redshift clusters. Masking clusters detected above a signal-to-noise threshold suppresses this contribution and shifts the sensitivity of the masked power spectrum towards lower-mass and higher-redshift halos. The resolved clusters removed from the map contribute to the cluster number count sample which probes the high-mass population above the survey detection threshold.

To explicitly compare which halos source each observable, we compute the
normalized differential contribution of halos of mass $M$ and redshift $z$
to each probe,
\begin{equation}
K_X(z,M)
=
\frac{1}{X}
\frac{\partial^2 X}
{\partial\ln z\,\partial\ln M},
\label{eq:kernel2d}
\end{equation}
for $X\in\{C_\ell^{yy},\,C_\ell^{yy,\mathrm{mask}},\,N\}$, normalized such
that $\int\mathrm{d}\ln z\,\mathrm{d}\ln M\,K_X=1$. For the tSZ power spectra we only consider the dominant one-halo term in both masked and unmasked case. Following Equation \ref{eq:masked_tsz_1h},
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 C_\ell^{yy}}
{\partial\ln z\,\partial\ln M}
=
z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}}{\mathrm{d}\ln M}\,
\left|\bar y_\ell(M,z)\right|^2
W(M,z),
\label{eq:kernel2d_ps}
\end{equation}
with $W=e^{2\sigma_{\ln Y}^{2}}$ for the total unmasked tSZ power spectrum
[cf.\ Eq.~\eqref{eq:full_tsz_1h_scatter}; a constant that cancels in
the normalization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kernel2d}] and $W=W_2$ for the
masked power spectrum. For the CNC, the differential contribution to the halo abundance is given by
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 N}
{\partial\ln z\,\partial\ln M}
=
z\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}\,
\frac{\mathrm{d}n_{\rm h}}{\mathrm{d}\ln M}\,
\hat{\chi}(M,z).
\label{eq:kernel2d_cnc}
\end{equation}
The kernels are evaluated at the fiducial cosmology with the mean
signal-to-noise ratio $\bar q_m(M,z)$ of Equation \ref{eq:mean_snr} computed
using the sky-averaged \textit{Planck} MMF noise, a detection threshold
$q_{\rm cat}=5$, and intrinsic scatter $\sigma_{\ln Y}=0.173$.

We also plot the contours of the mean signal-to-noise ratios, $\bar q_m(M,z)$, which represent the detection boundary that splits the halo population between the two probes, with halos above it entering the resolved cluster catalogue and halos below it remaining unresolved in the map. It is noticeable that, at high redshift, the selection contours bend towards lower masses. This is because $\bar q_m(M,z)$ is the ratio between the signal and the filter noise, and the Compton-$y$ signal grows as $\bar y_0\propto E^2(z)$ which does not dim with distance. Although the filter noise initially increases as the cluster angular size decreases, this growth weakens at high redshift because $D_A(z)$ reaches its maximum at $z\simeq1.6$ while $R_{500\mathrm{c}}$ shrinks only mildly. The angular size therefore evolves more slowly.  Because of the intrinsic scatter, the CNC and masked tSZ power-spectrum kernels extend slightly below and above, respectively, the mean selection boundary, $\bar q_m(M,z)=q_{\rm cat}$. This reflects that clusters near the detection threshold scatter above the detection limit and enter the catalogue, while clusters that scatter below it remains unresolved in the map.

On large angular scales, the tSZ power spectrum is dominated by rare, massive halos. In the Planck-relevant regime, these are largely the same clusters that enter the resolved cluster catalogue, so the total tSZ power spectrum and the cluster number counts probe strongly overlapping halo populations. This overlap is evident in Figure~\ref{fig:kernels2d_ell}, where the CNC contribution contours substantially overlap with the peak of the total tSZ power spectrum kernel at $\ell \sim 1000$. Masking the detected clusters suppresses this contribution and shifts the power-spectrum sensitivity towards lower-mass, unresolved halos, thereby reducing the overlap with the CNC sample and making the two observables more complementary. 

We also investigate the mean mass and redshift contribution of each probe. The mean mass and redshift dependence is given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle M_{500\mathrm{c}}\right\rangle_X(\ell)
&=
\int \mathrm{d}\ln z\,\mathrm{d}\ln M\,
M_{500\mathrm{c}}\,K_X(z,M;\ell),
\\
\left\langle z\right\rangle_X(\ell)
&=
\int \mathrm{d}\ln z\,\mathrm{d}\ln M\,
z\,K_X(z,M;\ell).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mean_mass_redshift}
\end{equation}

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
%==============================================================================
In this section, we describe the methods to test this joint analysis framework. We proceed in two stages. First, we generate synthetic \textit{Planck}-like data using the same halo-model ingredients as the theoretical framework. This controlled setting allows us to validate the analysis pipeline and assess its performance under internally consistent modelling assumptions. We then apply the same analysis to the FLAMINGO hydrodynamical simulations to test the framework when the tSZ signal is determined by simulated intracluster gas physics rather than by the parametric halo model.

\subsection{Synthetic data generation}
\label{subsec:syntheticdata}
We first test the joint analysis pipeline using synthetic data generated at a fixed set of cosmological and cluster-scaling parameters.
The fiducial values used to generate the mocks are
$\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.310$,
$\sigma_8=0.780$
(so that $S_8\equiv\sigma_8\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/0.3}=0.792$),
$H_0=67.66$,
$n_{\mathrm{s}}=0.9665$,
$\omega_{\mathrm{b}}=0.02242$,
hydrostatic mass bias $1-b=0.709$ (equivalently $B=1.41$),
$A_{\mathrm{SZ}}=-4.237$,
$\alpha_{\mathrm{SZ}}=1.12$,
and $\sigma_{\ln Y}=0.173$.
Mock halo catalogues are produced using the catalogue generator implemented in the publicly available \texttt{cosmocnc} package \citep{ZubeldiaBolliet2024}. At a given point in parameter space, mock cluster catalogues are generated by Poisson sampling of halo masses and redshifts from the HMF of \citet{Tinker:2008ff}. The HMF is evaluated with \texttt{hmfast}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/hmfast/hmfast/tree/main}}, using the matter power spectrum provided by the \texttt{cosmopower} emulator \citep{SpurioMancini2022}. The mass and redshift ranges of the halos are chosen to be \(10^{14}\,M_\odot \leq M_{500\mathrm{c}} \leq 10^{16}\,M_\odot\) and \(0.005\leq z\leq3\). Each halo is assigned a position drawn uniformly over the sky, such that halo clustering is neglected in the synthetic realizations. Then, for each cluster, the scaling relations of the mass observables are applied according to Equations \ref{eq:y0_scaling_relation} and \ref{eq:theta500_scaling_relation}. Intrinsic scatter in the tSZ signal and measurement noise are then applied successively to obtain the observed signal-to-noise ratio, $q_{\rm obs}$. Clusters satisfying $q_{\rm obs}\ge q_{\rm cat}$ are retained as the CNC sample and binned in redshift and signal-to-noise. We adopt a threshold of $q_{\rm cat}=5$ and use $10$ redshift bins spanning $0.005\le z\le 1$ together with $5$ signal-to-noise bins spanning $q_{\rm cat}\le q_{\rm obs}\le 40$. 
% The same $(z,q)$ bin edges are used when computing the expected counts in the likelihood, so that the synthetic catalogue data vector and the model prediction share a common definition.

Having generated the mock catalogue and assigned the cluster observables, we next construct the corresponding total Compton-$y$ maps. The maps are generated on a HEALPix grid with resolution $N_{\rm side}=1024$ \citep{Gorski2005}, using the \texttt{JXPaint}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/licongxu/JXPaint}} package. Each halo is assigned the A10 pressure profile shape and convolved with a Gaussian beam with $\theta_{\rm FWHM}= 10$ arcmin, with the amplitude of the pressure profile adjusted to reproduce the true value of $y_0$ obtained after intrinsic scatter is applied. The beam-convolved profile is painted within a finite angular radius with $\theta_{\rm max}=\max\!\left(4\theta_{500},\,2\theta_{\rm FWHM}\right)$, where $\theta_{500}=R_{500\mathrm{c}}/D_{\rm A}(z)$. This choice of truncation radius preserves the signal of resolved, extended clusters while ensuring that compact clusters are sampled over at least the angular scale of the beam. Since the pressure profile contributes negligibly at sufficiently large radii, the truncation has little effect on the total signal while reducing the computational cost of map generation. When residual foreground contamination is included, Gaussian random-field realizations of the CIB, infrared point-source, and radio-source components are generated from their angular power-spectrum templates and added to each painted Compton-$y$ signal map \citep{Planck2015XXII}. Note that this simplified assumption reproduces the assumed foreground residual power spectra but neglects their intrinsic non-Gaussianity and any correlations between components.

From these maps, we measure both the full-sky and masked tSZ power spectra. We adopt the multipole binning scheme used in the \textit{Planck} 2015 analysis, defining the bandpowers at the 18 effective multipoles, $\ell_{\rm eff}$, listed in Ref.~\citep{Planck2015XXII}. To compute the masked power spectrum, clusters with $q_{\rm obs}\ge q_{\rm cat}$ are masked from the map with circular holes of the same angular radius $\theta_{\rm max}$ used in the painting step, with a $0.25^\circ$ cosine taper apodization around the boundary of each hole. We use the MASTER pseudo-$C_\ell$ algorithm \citep{Hivon2002} as implemented in \texttt{NaMaster} \citep{Alonso2019} to compute the masked power spectrum. Within each bin, we apply uniform weights to $D_\ell$ when averaging over multipoles with the effective multipoles at the centers of each multipole bin. We validate the power spectra computed from synthetic maps against the corresponding halo-model predictions in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic_validation}, and the power spectrum distribution from synthetic maps is consistent with theoretical predictions.

\subsection{Application to FLAMINGO simulations}
\label{sec:methods_flamingo}
After demonstrating the joint CNC and masked power spectrum pipeline on synthetic data generated using the halo model, we next apply the same pipeline to maps generated from hydrodynamical simulations, which provide a more realistic description of cluster thermodynamics, baryonic feedback, and departures from the idealized halo model. For this purpose, we use the FLAMINGO suite of cosmological hydrodynamical simulations \citep{Schaye2023,Kugel2023}. Here, we provide a brief summary about the FLAMINGO simulations, and we refer the readers to \citet{Schaye2023} and \citet{Kugel2023} for more detailed descriptions. 

FLAMINGO is a large suite of hydrodynamical cosmological simulations covering large cosmic volumes. The simulations are performed with the open-source cosmology, gravity, hydrodynamics, and galaxy formation code \texttt{SWIFT} using the SPHENIX smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) scheme \citep{Schaller2024,Borrow2022}. The simulations are initialized using the modified version of \textsc{monofonIC} \citep{Hahn2021}, and simulations include neutrino particles using the $\delta f$ method \citep{Elbers2021}. An important feature of FLAMINGO simulations is the machine learning-assisted calibration of stellar and AGN feedback to the observed $z=0$ galaxy stellar mass function (SMF) and $z=0.1-0.3$ cluster gas fractions \citep{Kugel2023}. 

In this work, our main analysis uses the intermediate-resolution L1\_m9 simulations, with an initial mean baryonic particle mass $m_g=1.0 \times10^9 M_\odot$ and mean CDM particle mass $m_{\mathrm{CDM}}=5.65\times10^{9}\,M_\odot$ in a $(1\,\mathrm{cGpc})^3$ volume. These simulations use $N_{\mathrm{b}}=N_{\mathrm{CDM}}=1800^{3}$ gas and dark matter particles, and $N_\nu=1000^{3}$ neutrino particles. FLAMINGO also provides the L2p8\_m9 simulations, which adopt the same mass resolution, fiducial cosmology, and calibrated feedback model as L1\_m9, but cover a larger (2.8 cGpc)$^3$ volume and provide eight lightcones. \citet{McCarthy:2023ism} showed that the predicted tSZ power spectrum is generally robust to this increase in simulation volume, although the 1 cGpc boxes miss a small amount of power owing to incomplete sampling of rare, massive clusters, while lightcone-to-lightcone variance remains significant at $\ell\lesssim300$. We therefore use L1\_m9 for the main analysis and the controlled comparison of astrophysical variations. We use the fiducial FLAMINGO cosmology (D3A) which adopts parameter values from the
Dark Energy Survey Year~3 \citep{Abbott2022} $3\times2$pt plus external constraints
\citep{Schaye2023}:
$\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.306$, $\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}=0.0486$,
$\sigma_8=0.807$, $h=0.681$, $n_s=0.967$, and $\sum m_\nu=0.06\,\mathrm{eV}$. We also include the LS8 model which has a lower amplitude of the power spectrum, $S_8=0.766$, to investigate the cosmological variations of the signals. In addition to the fiducial D3A model, we also analyze a set of calibrated astrophysics variations at this resolution from the FLAMINGO suite. These include the \(f_{\rm gas}\pm N\sigma\) series, in which the cluster gas fractions used for calibration are shifted up or down by $N$ standard deviations relative to the observational constraints while the SMF is held fixed; the \(M_*-\sigma\) model, in which the observed stellar masses are decreased by the expected systematic uncertainty of \(0.14\,\mathrm{dex}\) prior to calibration; and the Jet models that replace the isotropic thermal AGN feedback with kinetic, collimated jets.

\subsubsection{Multifrequency FLAMINGO mock skies}
For the observational analysis, we use the multicomponent full-sky mock maps constructed from the FLAMINGO simulations by \citet{Yang:2025per}. These products are based on the FLAMINGO lightcone outputs and provide spatially self-consistent realizations of several secondary CMB and extragalactic foreground signals. We briefly summarize here the construction of the components most relevant to our analysis and refer to \citet{Yang:2025per} for a complete description.

The FLAMINGO lightcone maps are constructed by dividing the past lightcone of each observer into concentric spherical shells in comoving distance. Whenever a simulation particle crosses the past lightcone of an observer placed within the periodic simulation volume, its contribution is assigned to the corresponding radial shell and accumulated in the appropriate HEALPix pixel. The resulting shell maps can then be combined along the line of sight to produce integrated full-sky maps. Because the widths of the lightcone shells can exceed the simulation box size, some lines of sight may pass through multiple periodic copies of the same structures, introducing artificial correlations into the maps. We therefore use the rotated lightcone products, for which the shells are randomly rotated whenever the lightcone diameter exceeds a multiple of the box size. 

% \LX{Discuss the impact of periodic universe.}

The tSZ component is constructed directly from the thermodynamic properties of the gas particles in the FLAMINGO lightcones. For a gas particle intersecting the observer lightcone, its contribution to the Compton-\(y\) map is given by
\begin{equation}
\Delta y
=
\frac{\sigma_{\rm T} k_{\rm B}}{m_e c^2}
\frac{m_g n_e T_e}
{\Omega_{\rm pix} d_A^2 \rho},
\label{eq:flamingo_particle_y}
\end{equation}
where $m_g$, $\rho$, $n_e$, and $T_e$ are the mass, density, electron number density, and temperature of the gas particle. $d_A$ is the angular-diameter distance to the observer, and $\Omega_{\rm pix}$ is the pixel solid angle. The particle contributions are distributed over the sky using the SPH smoothing kernel and accumulated to construct the Compton-$y$ map.

The CIB maps are constructed from the star formation rate (SFR) lightcone maps predicted by FLAMINGO, since the CIB is strongly correlated with the distribution of star-forming galaxies. Following \citet{Yang:2025per},
the simulated SFR is converted to a bolometric infrared luminosity using
\begin{equation}
\frac{L_{\rm bol,IR}}{10^{10}L_\odot}
=
\frac{\mathrm{SFR}}
{1\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}}.
\label{eq:flamingo_cib_sfr}
\end{equation}
The bolometric infrared luminosity is converted to the luminosity at a given frequency by modelling the spectral energy distribution (SED) as a modified blackbody,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(\nu,z)
\propto
\frac{\nu^{\beta_d+3}}
{\exp\left[
h\nu/(k_{\rm B}T_{\rm dust})
\right]-1},
\label{eq:flamingo_cib_sed}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_d$ is the dust emissivity index and the dust temperature
evolves with redshift as $T_{\rm dust}=T_0(1+z)^\alpha$. The luminosity at a given rest-frame frequency is then obtained by
normalizing the SED to the bolometric infrared luminosity,
\begin{equation}
L_{\nu,{\rm IR}}
=
L_{\rm bol,IR}
\frac{\Phi(\nu,T_{\rm dust})}
{\int \mathrm{d}\nu\,\Phi(\nu,T_{\rm dust})}.
\label{eq:flamingo_cib_lnu}
\end{equation}
The corresponding observed flux density is then
\begin{equation}
S_{\nu,{\rm IR}}
=
\frac{
L_{\nu(1+z),{\rm IR}}
}{
4\pi \chi^2(z)(1+z)
},
\label{eq:flamingo_cib_flux}
\end{equation}
where $\chi(z)$ is the radial comoving distance, and the luminosity is
evaluated at the corresponding rest-frame frequency $\nu(1+z)$. The parameters
$\{\beta_d,T_0,\alpha\}$ are calibrated by \citet{Yang:2025per} against
the Planck CIB auto-power spectra at 353, 545, and 857 GHz.

The primary CMB is generated as a Gaussian random field from a theoretical CMB temperature power spectrum computed at the corresponding cosmological parameter of each FLAMINGO map. The same CMB realization is used across all frequency channels in thermodynamic CMB temperature units. We add independent homogeneous white instrumental noise to the six frequency channels corresponding to the Planck HFI bands, adopting the channel-dependent beam FWHM and noise levels summarized in Table~\ref{tab:planck_mock_properties}. The multifrequency mock map at frequency $\nu$ can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
d_{\nu}(\hat{\bm n})
=
B_{\nu}\star
\left[
T_{\rm CMB}(\hat{\bm n})
+
T_{{\rm tSZ},\nu}(\hat{\bm n})
+
T_{{\rm CIB},\nu}(\hat{\bm n})
\right]
+
n_{\nu}(\hat{\bm n}),
\label{eq:multifrequency_mock_map}
\end{equation}
where $B_{\nu}$ denotes convolution with the Gaussian instrumental beam, $T_{\rm CMB}$ is the primary CMB realization, $T_{{\rm tSZ},\nu}$ and $T_{{\rm CIB},\nu}$ are the frequency-dependent tSZ and CIB contributions, respectively, and $n_{\nu}$ is the instrumental-noise realization. 

\subsubsection{Cluster detection and power spectrum reconstruction}

We extract the cluster catalogue and the tSZ power spectrum directly from the multifrequency mock skies using two complementary pipelines. Resolved clusters are identified using the MMF approach, while the Compton-$y$ field is reconstructed using the HILC approach. Both analyses use the same six \textit{Planck} HFI frequency channels at 100, 143, 217, 353, 545, and 857 GHz.

Following the \texttt{SZiFi} cluster-finding pipeline, we tessellate the sky into 768 non-overlapping selection tiles defined by a much coarser HEALPix pixelization with $N_{\rm side}=8$. Each selection tile covers $53.7\,{\rm deg}^2$, corresponding to a characteristic angular extent of approximately $7.3^\circ$. For the actual MMF calculation, a $14.8^\circ\times14.8^\circ$ square cutout of each frequency map is constructed around the centre of each selection tile and projected onto a $1024\times1024$ flat-sky grid, corresponding to a pixel size of $0.867\,\mathrm{arcmin}$. The cutouts are deliberately larger than the selection tiles so that each HEALPix tile is fully contained within its corresponding flat-sky field.

Within each field, we filter the frequency maps using the non-relativistic tSZ spectral dependence and the projected pressure profile of \citet{Arnaud2010}, convolved with the \textit{Planck} instrumental beams. To optimize the detection of clusters of different angular size, the MMF is evaluated at 25 logarithmically spaced trial values of $\theta_{500}$ between $0.5$ and $32\,\mathrm{arcmin}$, using Fourier modes in the range $100 \leq \ell \leq 2500$. For each trial scale, we construct maps of $\hat{y}_0$ and $q$. The set of signal-to-noise maps defines a three-dimensional field, $q(\boldsymbol{x};\theta_{500})$, spanning the two sky coordinates and the filter angular scale. Cluster candidates are identified as local peaks of this three-dimensional signal-to-noise field. For each candidate, we retain the position and filter scale that give the highest signal-to-noise ratio. We denote this optimized detection significance by $q_{\rm opt} = \hat{y}_0/\sigma_{\hat{y}_0}$, where both the recovered central Compton-$y$ amplitude and its uncertainty are evaluated at the selected position and filter scale.

For each field, \texttt{SZiFi} estimates the cross-channel noise covariance matrix from the multifrequency maps. Note that if the resolved clusters themselves are included when estimating the noise covariance matrix, the MMF noise can be overestimated and the recovered signal-to-noise ratio suppressed. We therefore use the iterative MMF (iMMF) procedure of \citet{Zubeldia_2023}. After an initial cluster search, detections with $q>4$ are masked within $3\theta_{500}$, the noise covariance is re-estimated from the masked maps, and the matched-filter search is repeated using the updated noise covariance. Detections arising from overlapping tiles are subsequently merged, and the final resolved cluster sample is defined by $q_{\rm opt}\geq q_{\rm cat}=5$.

The spatial correlation between the CIB and the tSZ signal can bias the signal-to-noise ratio and the CNC. To mitigate this effect, we adopt the spectrally constrained iterative multifrequency matched filter (sciMMF) introduced in \citet{2023MNRAS.522.5123Z}. The sciMMF extends the standard iMMF by imposing an additional spectral constraint that deprojects a specified CIB SED. This suppresses contamination from the cluster-correlated CIB, at the cost of an increase in the MMF noise. We use the default \texttt{SZiFi}/\textit{Planck} CIB model to deproject the CIB. Unless otherwise stated, CNC and the cluster mask use this sciMMF sample.

Independently, we reconstruct the Compton-$y$ map from the same multifrequency maps using HILC. We perform the reconstruction for both the total and cluster-masked skies. For the latter, clusters detected by sciMMF with $q_{\rm opt}\geq q_{\rm cat}$ are masked in each frequency map with a masking radius of $\max(4\theta_{500},2\theta_{\rm FWHM})$, and the HILC weights are recomputed from the masked multifrequency maps. To avoid instrumental-noise bias in the reconstructed power spectrum, we construct two sets of multifrequency maps containing the same sky signal but independent noise realizations. The HILC is then performed independently for the two sets of maps for both the total and masked cases, and we estimate the corresponding tSZ power spectra from the cross-spectrum of the two reconstructed Compton-$y$ maps.

\subsection{Likelihood Analysis}
\label{sec:methods_likelihood}

Parameter inference is performed with Markov-chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling using \texttt{Cobaya} \citep{TorradoLewis2021}. Here we introduce the likelihood we used for each probe and the priors adopted in the synthetic-data inference.

\paragraph{Cluster number counts.}
For the CNC we adopt a binned Poisson likelihood in redshift and signal-to-noise, as implemented in the \texttt{cosmocnc} package \citep{ZubeldiaBolliet2024}. Given observed counts $N^{\mathrm{obs}}_{ij}$ in the redshift bin $[z_i,z_{i+1}]$ and SNR bin $[q_j,q_{j+1}]$, and the theoretical expectations $N_{ij}$ from Equation~\ref{eq:joint_cluster_counts},
\begin{equation}
\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CNC}}
=
\sum_{ij}
\left[
N^{\mathrm{obs}}_{ij}\ln N_{ij}
-
N_{ij}
-
\ln\!\left(N^{\mathrm{obs}}_{ij}!\right)
\right].
\label{eq:lnLCNC}
\end{equation}
The predicted number counts $N_{ij}$ are computed with the same observable--mass relations, selection function, and completeness used to construct the catalogue, so that the CNC likelihood is fully consistent with the forward model of Secs.~\ref{sec:joint_analysis} and~\ref{subsec:syntheticdata}.

\paragraph{tSZ power spectrum.}
For the binned tSZ power spectrum we adopt a Gaussian likelihood,
\begin{equation}
-2\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PS}}
=
\left(\hat{\bm{D}}-\bm{D}\right)^{\mathsf T}
\bm{M}^{-1}
\left(\hat{\bm{D}}-\bm{D}\right)
+\ln|\bm{M}|
+\mathrm{const.},
\label{eq:lnLPS}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bm{D}}$ is the measured bandpower vector in the $18$ multipole bins of Sec.~\ref{subsec:syntheticdata}, $\bm{D}$ is the theoretical prediction (full-sky or masked according to the case under study), and $\bm{M}$ is the bandpower covariance. We use a fixed covariance matrix computed at the fiducial cosmology. The covariance is estimated from the ensemble of 1000 mock maps described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:syntheticdata}, with the Hartlap correction applied to debias the inverse sample covariance \citep{Hartlap2007}. We have verified that the resulting covariance is consistent with the analytic prediction of Eq.~\ref{eq:masked_bandpower_covariance}.

The theoretical bandpower prediction depends on whether residual foreground contamination is included in the data. For synthetic data without residual foregrounds, $\bm{D}=\bm{D}^{\mathrm{tSZ}}$, where $\bm{D}^{\mathrm{tSZ}}$ is the (masked or full-sky) binned halo-model tSZ prediction alone. When the residual foregrounds are included, residual foreground components are included as fixed bandpower templates with free amplitude parameters,
\begin{equation}
\bm{D}
\simeq
\bm{D}^{\mathrm{tSZ}}
+
A_{\mathrm{CIB}}\,\bm{D}^{\mathrm{CIB}}
+
A_{\mathrm{IR}}\,\bm{D}^{\mathrm{IR}}
+
A_{\mathrm{RS}}\,\bm{D}^{\mathrm{RS}},
\label{eq:fg_model}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{D}^{\mathrm{CIB}}$, $\bm{D}^{\mathrm{IR}}$, and $\bm{D}^{\mathrm{RS}}$ are fixed residual templates for the cosmic infrared background (CIB), infrared point sources (IR), and radio sources (RS), binned into the same $18$ multipole windows as the tSZ model, while $A_{\mathrm{CIB}}$, $A_{\mathrm{IR}}$, and $A_{\mathrm{RS}}$ are the amplitudes of each template.

\paragraph{Cross-covariance between CNC and the tSZ power spectrum.}
The CNC and the total tSZ power spectrum are in principle correlated because resolved clusters contribute to both observables, and the total tSZ power spectrum is sensitive to massive local clusters which are the most readily detected. Masking all clusters with $q_{\rm obs}>q_{\rm cat}$ removes this shared population, so that the CNC and masked power spectrum probe disjoint sets of clusters and their cross-covariance is strongly suppressed. In Figure~\ref{fig:cnc_ps_corr_mc}, we estimate the correlation between the CNC and tSZ power spectrum from the $1000$ synthetic realizations with matched mock catalogues. The resulting correlation matrices show a non-zero correlation between the CNC and the total tSZ bandpowers, whereas no statistically significant correlation is detected between the CNC and the masked tSZ bandpowers. Nevertheless, we neglect the cross-covariance in both combinations and approximate the joint likelihood as the product of the individual CNC and power-spectrum likelihoods,
\begin{equation}
\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{joint}}
=
\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PS}}
+
\ln\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CNC}}.
\label{eq:jointlkl}
\end{equation}
This approximation is well justified for the masked power spectrum. For the total power spectrum, we follow \citet{Salvati:2018} which assumes that the covariance between the CNC and tSZ power spectrum measurements can be neglected.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
%==============================================================================
We now present the results from our joint analysis pipeline.
We first show constraints from synthetic \textit{Planck}-like mocks generated with the same halo-model ingredients as the forward model (Sec.~\ref{subsec:syntheticdata}), and subsequently apply the same analysis to FLAMINGO hydrodynamical simulations. On the synthetic data we run five inference setups:
\begin{itemize}
    \item CNC-only,
    \item masked tSZ power spectrum only,
    \item full-sky tSZ power spectrum only,
    \item joint CNC~$+$~masked tSZ power spectrum 
    \item joint CNC~$+$~full-sky tSZ power spectrum.
\end{itemize}
Each setup is applied to a single catalogue realization selected from the ensemble of $1000$ mocks and to its corresponding painted Compton-$y$ map.

\subsection{Synthetic mocks without residual foregrounds}
\label{sec:results_syn_nofg}
Figure~\ref{fig:triangle_signal_single} compares the CNC-only, full-sky tSZ, and masked tSZ posteriors in the signal-only case, with the corresponding marginalized constraints summarized in the upper block of Table~\ref{tab:constraints_syn}. The CNC analysis provides substantially tighter constraints than the full-sky tSZ power spectrum. This reflects the additional information retained by binning the cluster catalogue in $q$ and $z$ bins, which captures the mass- and redshift-dependent evolution of the halo abundance. The power spectrum therefore exhibits a degeneracy among $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$, $\sigma_8$, $A_{\rm SZ}$, and $\alpha_{\rm SZ}$, which controls the overall amplitude of the tSZ power spectrum.

Masking the detected clusters leads to significantly tighter cosmological constraints on the tSZ power spectrum than using the total tSZ power spectrum, reducing the marginalized uncertainties by factors of approximately $5.4$ for $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$, $4.8$ for $\sigma_8$, and $7.0$ for $S_8$. The full-sky tSZ signal is dominated by a relatively small number of massive and local clusters, which generate a large connected trispectrum contribution and hence substantial non-Gaussian sample variance. Masking these rare objects suppresses this covariance, leaving a masked spectrum sourced by the more numerous unresolved cluster population, whose aggregate signal distribution is closer to Gaussian. In the signal-only analysis, the resulting reduction in variance more than compensates for the loss of total tSZ power, thereby increasing the effective information content of the measured spectrum.

\subsection{Synthetic mocks with residual foregrounds}
\label{sec:results_syn_fg}
We next repeat the synthetic-data analysis after including residual CIB, IR, and RS foreground components, while allowing their template amplitudes $A_{\mathrm{CIB}}$, $A_{\mathrm{IR}}$, and $A_{\mathrm{RS}}$ to vary.
The CNC likelihood is unchanged, since the catalogue is built from the detection significance derived from the MMF analysis.
Figure~\ref{fig:triangle_fg_single} and the lower block of
Table~\ref{tab:constraints_syn} summarize the cosmological and residual-amplitude
constraints after marginalizing over the residual templates.

Marginalizing over the foreground amplitudes substantially degrades the
constraining power of both tSZ power-spectrum measurements, and masking giant
clusters improves the determination of the residual amplitudes themselves
instead of the cosmological parameters
(Table~\ref{tab:constraints_syn}).
Masking tightens the constraint on $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ by a factor of
approximately $1.3$, while the constraints on $\sigma_8$ are comparable and
that on $S_8$ is weaker by a factor of approximately $1.6$.
By contrast, the marginalized uncertainties are reduced by factors of
approximately $4.6$, $1.7$, and $1.9$ for $A_{\mathrm{CIB}}$, $A_{\mathrm{IR}}$,
and $A_{\mathrm{RS}}$, respectively, for the masked power-spectrum measurement.
In our model, we assume that the foreground residuals remain unchanged after
masking.
Masking therefore removes part of the tSZ signal while leaving the foreground
residuals unaffected, increasing their relative contribution to the measured
spectrum and also the degeneracy between the masked tSZ signal and the
foreground templates.
Consequently, the information gained from the reduced covariance is absorbed
predominantly by the foreground-amplitude constraints rather than by the
cosmological parameters.

and $S_8$ by factors of approximately $1.1$, $1.2$, and $1.2$, respectively,
while the CNC~$+$~masked combination yields improvements of approximately
$1.1$ for all three parameters.
The cosmological constraints from both joint analyses are therefore comparable
and remain dominated by CNC, with little additional gain from the power
spectrum.
By contrast, adding CNC to the masked power spectrum reduces the uncertainties
on $A_{\mathrm{CIB}}$, $A_{\mathrm{IR}}$, and $A_{\mathrm{RS}}$ by factors of
approximately $1.3$, $2.0$, and $1.9$, respectively, relative to the masked
power-spectrum-only analysis
(Table~\ref{tab:constraints_syn}).
Thus, in the presence of residual foregrounds, the principal improvement from
the joint CNC~$+$~masked-spectrum analysis is the constraints on foreground
residual amplitudes, rather than the cosmological constraints.

\subsection{Cluster detection and the masked tSZ power spectrum in FLAMINGO}
\label{sec:flamingo_fiducial}
The synthetic analyses above show that the gain from masking resolved clusters is largely absorbed by residual-foreground amplitudes once those amplitudes are marginalized over. We now extend this analysis to the FLAMINGO hydrodynamical simulations, where the maps are generated directly from the simulated gas distribution, and clusters are detected from mock multifrequency maps with \texttt{SZiFi} rather than assigned at known halo positions.

The FLAMINGO mock maps include CIB emission that is spatially correlated with the tSZ signal, which can bias the signal-to-noise ratio obtained by the MMF. To study this correlation, we construct a controlled sky map in
which only the CIB is randomized. We divide the CIB maps into the same
768 HEALPix tiles of $N_{\mathrm{side}}=8$ used for cluster finding,
draw a random permutation of those tiles, and apply that permutation
to the CIB at every frequency, leaving the CMB, tSZ, and noise in place. This preserves the CIB auto-power and SED while removing its spatial correlation with the tSZ. Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_cnc_cib_randomized} shows the resulting binned CNC. For the iMMF, randomizing the CIB leads to an increase in CNC in several of the bins, which is larger than the corresponding Poisson errors. In contrast, the sciMMF catalogue is much more stable across the bins. This indicates that, for the FLAMINGO mocks considered here, cluster-correlated CIB can have a non-negligible impact on the recovered CNC, while the sciMMF reduces this sensitivity to CIB contamination. We therefore adopt the sciMMF catalogue to define the cluster masks used in the remainder of the analysis. Note that this impact depends on the experimental configuration, and the randomization test cannot be performed directly on real observations.

We then study the impact of cluster masking on the tSZ power spectrum on the underlying noiseless Compton-$y$ maps. Using the sciMMF catalogue, we
progressively mask clusters with $q_{\rm opt}>50$, $20$, $10$, and $6$ at radius $\max(4\theta_{500},2\theta_{\rm FWHM})$, and measure $D_\ell^{yy}$ with the MASTER estimator used for the synthetic data. Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_fiducial_masked_ps} shows the result. Masking
five $q>50$ clusters already removes about half of the power at large scales, and lowering the threshold to $q>6$ leaves only
$\simeq18\%$ of the full-sky bandpower at large scales, while the same mask
removes $\simeq37\%$ at $\ell\sim1000$. The suppression due to cluster masking weakens toward small scales as expected, because the small-scale signal is dominated by the lower mass halos that remain unmasked as shown in Sec.~\ref{sec:kernels}. We also compare the total and masked power spectra for $q_{\rm cat}=6$ with the \textit{Planck} tSZ power-spectrum measurements of \citet{Bolliet_2018} and the masked tSZ power spectrum of \citet{Rotti_2021}. The total tSZ power spectrum lies systematically above the \textit{Planck} measurements from \citet{Bolliet_2018}, consistent with the findings of \citet{McCarthy:2023ism}, who argued that this excess favours a lower clustering amplitude rather than extreme baryonic feedback. Interestingly, after masking clusters with $q\geq6$, the masked power spectrum is lower than the masked measurement of \citet{Rotti_2021}. This should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously, since the \textit{Planck} data is derived from component-separated $y$-maps and sensitive to the modelling of residual foregrounds. \citet{Rotti_2021} found that masking also changes the degeneracy between the inferred tSZ and CIB amplitudes, possibly because of CIB--tSZ correlations associated with the resolved cluster population. Differences in the resolved cluster populations, selection functions, and foreground treatment can therefore all affect the comparison. It does not by itself establish improved agreement with the underlying \textit{Planck} tSZ signal.

We next reconstruct the Compton-$y$ maps with HILC and compare their independent-noise cross-spectra with the noiseless tSZ spectrum evaluated using the same mask. Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_hilc_recovered} shows the five deprojection choices (no deprojection, CIB, CIB$+\delta \beta$, CIB$+\delta \beta+$CMB, and CIB$+\delta \beta + \delta T$). Applying the fitted weights to isolated CIB and CMB maps gives the component residual cross-spectra. The plotted uncertainties combine the approximate split-cross Gaussian variance with the model one-halo trispectrum.

We first compare the reconstructed power spectra with the ground truth on the unmasked and masked skies. On the unmasked sky, the reconstructed tSZ power spectra agree well with the input signal on large scales across the different deprojection prescriptions. At $\ell\simeq960$, the CIB residual is approximately $61\%$ of the true tSZ power without deprojection and $32\%$ with CIB deprojection. The corresponding recovered-to-true ratios are $1.44$ and $1.48$: reducing the isolated CIB residual does not necessarily reduce the net bias because other residual and cross terms also change. Adding the CIB spectral-index constraint gives a ratio of $1.27$, and additionally deprojecting the CMB lowers it to $1.10$. Deprojecting the temperature moment instead strongly suppresses the CIB residual but produces a reconstruction dominated by CMB leakage and noise at high multipoles. 

After masking the $q>5$ detections, the true tSZ power is suppressed more strongly than the residual CIB power. Consequently, on small scales the CIB residual without deprojection becomes comparable to the true masked tSZ signal, and the reconstructed-to-true ratio reaches $1.85$ at $\ell\simeq960$. This ratio decreases to $1.44$ with CIB deprojection, $1.27$ with CIB$+\delta\beta$ deprojection, and $1.18$ with CIB$+\delta\beta+$CMB deprojection. At $\ell\simeq10$, the corresponding ratios are $0.53$, $0.71$, $1.96$, and $1.87$, respectively. The CIB$+\delta\beta+\delta T$ reconstruction leads to a large variance penalty, with error bars approximately twenty times larger in the first multipole bin and six times larger in the last bin than in the reconstruction without deprojection. These results highlight the trade-off between foreground suppression and reconstruction variance in deprojections.

Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_hilc_cib_full_vs_masked} compares the CIB residual auto-spectrum before and after $q>5$ masking for the same five deprojections. Each curve applies that sky's own HILC weights to the isolated CIB maps. Without deprojection, masking reduces the residual to 29\% of its unmasked value at $\ell \simeq 10$. The ratio rises toward smaller scales and is 0.96 at $\ell \simeq 960$, so the last bin is almost unchanged. The stronger suppression on large scales may reflect the correlated CIB contributions from resolved large clusters. With CIB deprojection the masked residual is instead larger on large scales, by a factor of 1.8 at $\ell \simeq 10$ and 2.2 at $\ell \simeq 14$, and smaller on small scales, falling to 0.69 of the unmasked residual near $\ell \simeq 570$ and 0.83 at $\ell \simeq 960$. Adding the spectral-index constraint, with or without CMB deprojection, produces nearly the same residual and raises it by a factor of about 40 at $\ell \simeq 10$ and about 65 at $\ell \simeq 14$; at $\ell \simeq 960$ the masked residual remains higher, by factors of 1.5 and 1.4. Deprojecting the temperature moment as well gives the smallest unmasked residual at high multipoles, but the masked spectrum lies above its unmasked counterpart in every bin. For $\ell \lesssim 70$ it also exceeds the masked residual with no deprojection. 

With CIB deprojection, the residual remains nearly unchanged on large scales after masking and decreases slightly on small scales. With moment deprojection, masking has very little effect on the CIB residual, with the masked and unmasked spectra remaining nearly identical across the multipole range considered. The interpretation becomes less direct once deprojection is applied, since the additional constraints modify the HILC weights and hence the effective spectral and spatial response to the CIB. Overall, the CIB residual becomes progressively less sensitive to masking as additional CIB degrees of freedom are deprojected.

\subsection{Impact of astrophysical variations on the cluster counts and masked tSZ power spectrum}
\label{subsec:feedback_flamingo}
The synthetic analyses demonstrate that masking resolved clusters can substantially reduce the non-Gaussian covariance of the tSZ power spectrum, although the corresponding gain in cosmological information is strongly degraded once residual foreground amplitudes are marginalized over. We therefore ask how the observables respond to the variations in the astrophysical model in the FLAMINGO simulations at fixed cosmology.

We first compare the sciMMF catalogues for the different astrophysical prescriptions. Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_feedback_cnc} shows the binned CNC in five logarithmic bins over $5<q_{\rm opt}<40$ for the fiducial L1\_m9 sky and for the $f_{\rm gas}-8\sigma$ and $M_\star-1\sigma$ variations. The $f_{\rm gas}-8\sigma$ catalogue contains fewer clusters across the bins than the fiducial catalogue. This is possibly because lowering cluster gas fractions requires stronger AGN feedback, which reduces the integrated Compton-$y$ signal at the cluster center, and therefore the number of clusters that exceed $q_{\rm opt}=5$ decreases. In contrast, the $M_\star-1\sigma$ variation produces a modest increase in the CNC across the bins. Lowering the calibrated stellar masses leaves a larger fraction of baryons in the gas phase, increasing the thermal pressure of the ICM and hence the SZ signal of some halos.

Before considering the multifrequency reconstruction of the tSZ power spectrum using HILC, we first consider the (masked) tSZ power spectra computed on the noiseless Compton-$y$ maps. Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_truth_yy_ratio} shows the ratios of the total and $q>5$ masked tSZ power spectra for the $f_{\rm gas}-8\sigma$ and $M_\star-1\sigma$ models relative to the fiducial L1\_m9 case. We also show the relative uncertainties of the unmasked and masked signal-only power spectra for the L1\_m9 fiducial case as shaded bands, including both the Gaussian term and the one-halo trispectrum. The substantially narrower band after masking reflects the reduction of non-Gaussian trispectrum contribution when resolved clusters are masked.

The $f_{\rm gas}-8\sigma$ model produces a strongly scale-dependent response, with enhanced power at low multipoles and suppressed power at higher multipoles. The high-$\ell$ suppression is consistent with stronger AGN feedback redistributing hot gas from halo centres to larger radii, particularly in groups and lower-mass clusters with shallower potential wells, thereby broadening the pressure profiles and reducing small-scale power. The enhancement towards low multipoles may partly reflect the contribution of displaced hot gas to a more extended pressure distribution. Note that previous FLAMINGO studies similarly find that stronger feedback suppresses the integrated Compton-$y$ signal within $R_{500c}$, while boost the large-scale bias-weighted pressure \citep{Braspenning2024}. At high redshift, it can also enhance the signal integrated within $5R_{\rm 500c}$, where the correlated two-halo gas contributes \citep{Salcido2026}. Moreover, for the masked power spectrum, the response is further modified by number of resolved clusters being masked: stronger feedback leaves fewer clusters above the $q>5$ masking threshold, increasing the contribution from clusters that remain in the unresolved population. This tends to increase the unresolved power, while it also competes with the feedback-induced suppression of the halo mass function, which reduces the abundance of halos at a given mass.

The ratio obtained for the $M_\star-1\sigma$ variation shows a few-percent enhancement in the tSZ power spectrum, with only weak dependence on scale. This nearly scale-independent response suggests a a modest increase in the overall amplitude of the thermal pressure profile rather than a change in the shape. Shifting the SMF calibration towards lower stellar masses suppresses the conversion of cooling gas into stars through stronger stellar feedback, leaving a larger fraction of baryons in the hot gas phase in the ICM and thereby increasing the Compton-$y$ signal. This interpretation is also consistent with the increase in resolved number of clusters compared to fiducial model as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_feedback_cnc}. This enhancement is unlikely to be driven by cluster selection, since an increase in Compton-$y$ signal would result in more clusters being masked and would instead tend to reduce the residual tSZ power.

We next examine how these astrophysical responses propagate through the multifrequency HILC reconstruction and investigate the impact of foreground residuals on the reconstructed power spectra. For each variation, we compute the HILC split-cross bandpowers and quantify the resulting change relative to the fiducial noiseless tSZ signal using the fractional difference. Figure~\ref{fig:flamingo_hilc_feedback_ratio} shows this ratio for the unmasked sky with no deprojection and for the $q>5$ masked sky with no deprojection, CIB deprojection, CIB$+\delta\beta$, CIB$+\delta\beta+$CMB, and CIB$+\delta\beta+\delta T$. The shaded bands show the $1\sigma$ relative uncertainty of the corresponding fiducial reconstruction, with $\sigma^2=\sigma_G^2+\sigma_T^2$, where $\sigma_G$ is the split-cross Gaussian uncertainty of the HILC $y$ map and $\sigma_T$ is the one-halo trispectrum. Compared with the noiseless case, the Gaussian contribution is larger because the reconstructed maps contain noise and foreground residuals, which leads to larger bandpower variance even in the split-cross spectra. Different deprojections introduce additional noise penalties as expected. On the masked sky, CIB deprojection adds little noise and lies close to the no-deprojection band over most multipoles, though it is about $40\%$ wider at $\ell\simeq10$. Deprojecting CIB$+\delta\beta$ widens it by about a factor of three at $\ell\lesssim300$ and by about $30\%$ at higher $\ell$, and adding the CMB constraint leaves that band essentially unchanged. Deprojecting the additional CIB temperature moment, CIB$+\delta\beta+\delta T$, inflates the band substantially, by about a factor of thirteen at $\ell\simeq10$.

We also plot the level of foreground residuals, computed as the difference between the reconstructed HILC power spectrum and the corresponding noiseless tSZ power spectrum for each feedback variation, normalized by the fiducial noiseless tSZ power spectrum. In several cases, the foreground residuals are comparable to or larger than the statistical uncertainties of the HILC measurement. This indicates that foreground contamination may become a limiting systematic when using the reconstructed tSZ power spectrum to distinguish between feedback models, and distinguishing between different feedback models requires accurate modelling of the foreground residuals.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================
We have developed and tested a self-consistent framework for joint cluster
number counts and masked tSZ power spectrum analysis. Clusters detected above a signal-to-noise threshold, $q_{\rm cat}$, enter the CNC likelihood and are masked from the Compton-$y$ map, while the residual unresolved tSZ field provides the power spectrum constraint. The pipeline was first validated on \textit{Planck}-like painted mocks generated from the halo model. We subsequently applied the framework to multifrequency mock observations with FLAMINGO hydrodynamical simulations to investigate the sensitivity of CNC and the masked power spectrum to the foreground and different astrophysical models.

The main conclusions of this study are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Masking resolved clusters reduces the non-Gaussianity of the tSZ bandpowers at low multipoles, improving the detection significance of the tSZ power spectrum. The total tSZ power spectrum is highly skewed at low multipoles, and the variance is dominated by the one-halo trispectrum due to the Poisson sampling of the rare, massive halos. The masked power spectrum is sourced by a more numerous population of lower-mass halos and the bandpower
    distribution becomes approximately Gaussian in every bin, and the one-halo trispectrum term is significantly suppressed. In addition, the CNC and the total tSZ power spectrum are sourced from a similar halo population, whereas the masked tSZ power spectrum is sensitive to less massive halos. The CNC and masked power spectrum therefore probe more distinct and complementary halo populations.

    \item The binned CNC alone already provides tight cosmological constraints by retaining the redshift and signal-to-noise information of the resolved cluster population. In the signal-only synthetic analysis, the masked tSZ power spectrum is substantially more constraining than the full-sky spectrum and yields even tighter cosmological constraints than the binned CNC. Combining the masked spectrum with CNC further improves the constraints relative to CNC alone, whereas adding the full-sky spectrum provides only a modest gain in constraining power.

    \item Residual foreground contamination changes this conclusion. When the residual CIB, IR, and RS amplitudes are allowed to vary, foreground marginalization significantly degrades the cosmological constraints from both the total and masked tSZ power spectra. Masking then improves primarily the determination of the foreground amplitudes rather than the cosmological parameters. This arises because in our simplified assumption, masking suppresses the tSZ signal while leaving the assumed foreground residuals unchanged, and also partly because of the masked tSZ power spectrum is more degenerate in shape with the foreground templates. In the joint analysis between CNC and masked tSZ power spectrum, CNC constrains the cosmological parameters independently of the map-level foregrounds, thereby breaking the foreground--cosmology degeneracy and improving the determination of the residual foreground amplitudes, while the cosmological constraints remain dominated by CNC.
    
    \item The FLAMINGO multifrequency mocks show that foreground contamination also affects how both observables are extracted. Cluster-correlated CIB emission produces a noticeable change in the cluster counts recovered with the standard iterative matched filter, whereas the spectrally constrained filter is substantially more stable and is therefore adopted to construct the cluster masks. Progressive masking preferentially removes large-scale tSZ power, consistent with the halo-model picture inferred from the synthetic analysis. For the reconstructed Compton-$y$ field using HILC, masking suppresses the true tSZ signal more strongly than CIB residuals, increasing their relative importance. Additional HILC deprojection constraints can suppress CIB residuals further at the cost of increasing the variance, revealing a trade-off between foreground removal and statistical precision.
    
    \item At fixed cosmology, the FLAMINGO astrophysical variations produce detectable changes in both the cluster counts and the reconstructed masked tSZ power spectrum. The $f_{\rm gas}-8\sigma$ model produces fewer detected clusters and a scale-dependent power spectrum response, with enhanced large-scale power and suppression toward higher multipoles after masking. The $M_\star-1\sigma$ model instead produces a modest increase in the cluster counts and a few-percent large-scale enhancement in the no-deprojection and CIB-deprojected spectra. Masking suppresses the trispectrum error, so these noiseless differences are more significant than on the unmasked sky. In the HILC reconstructions, the foreground residual is in several cases comparable to or larger than the statistical uncertainty. Foreground contamination can therefore become a limiting systematic when the reconstructed spectrum is used to distinguish feedback models, and that distinction requires accurate modelling of the foreground residuals.
\end{itemize}

Several limitations should be kept in mind when interpreting these results. The cosmological inference is performed on deliberately controlled synthetic realizations, in which the halo population is generated without spatial clustering and the residual CIB, infrared-source, and radio-source components are modelled as Gaussian random fields with fixed power-spectrum templates. Our results show that the cosmological information recovered after foreground residual amplitude marginalization is sensitive to the shapes of foreground residuals. In a realistic analysis, the shapes of foreground residuals will depend on the component-separation procedure and can themselves change after cluster masking. A quantitative application to observations will require the foreground modelling and component-separation response to be propagated consistently through the joint analysis. The FLAMINGO analysis provides a more realistic complementary test by extracting both the cluster catalogue and the reconstructed Compton-$y$ field directly from spatially correlated multifrequency mock skies. These results nevertheless remain specific to the simulated astrophysical and foreground prescriptions considered within FLAMINGO framework, while applications to real data will involve additional observational and component-separation complexity.

To our knowledge, this is the first explicit demonstration of a joint CNC and masked tSZ power spectrum analysis based entirely on tSZ observables. It is also the first application of cluster masking to the FLAMINGO simulations, enabling the unresolved tSZ power spectrum and its response to different astrophysical prescriptions to be studied directly in large-volume hydrodynamical simulations. Although the analysis relies on synthetic data and simulations under simplified assumptions, it establishes a foundation for future joint analyses, clarifies the statistical properties and physical origin of the unresolved tSZ signal, and identifies the main modelling challenges that must be addressed before applying the framework to real survey data.

We conclude that a joint analysis of CNC and the masked tSZ power spectrum provides a promising way towards extracting complete information from the Compton-$y$ maps. Its practical implementation will require an end-to-end treatment of component separation, cluster detection and masking, and astrophysical modelling. These requirements can be tested with existing hydrodynamical simulations and \textit{Planck} data, and will remain central to applications with future CMB surveys.
